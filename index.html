Nepdine

Restaurant management system

Nepdine is a restaurant management system designed to simplify everyday restaurant operations through a clear and intuitive interface. The system brings key tasks such as table reservations, order management, menu management, staff coordination, and customer interactions into one streamlined platform. As the UI/UX designer, I focused on creating a clean, efficient, and easy-to-navigate experience, with clear information hierarchy and consistent interactions that help restaurant staff manage tasks quickly and effectively.

Role
UI/UX Designer — sole designer on the project
Dates
07/2025 – 12/2025 · 6 months
Platform
Responsive web · owner-facing site and staff-facing product
Scope
Table reservations, order processing, menu management, staff coordination, customer experience
Tools
Figma, Illustrator

Draft copy — edit freely

The problem

Restaurants here run on paper and memory. A host holds the reservation book, the kitchen works from handwritten tickets, and the manager finds out what happened a week later. Digitising that is not one job — it is four jobs that share one set of data and have completely different tolerances for friction.

How I worked

  • Mapped the service flow before drawing anything, and found the spine: reservation becomes table becomes order becomes bill. Everything else hangs off that chain.
  • Split the product into two vocabularies. The marketing surface talks to the owner, who is buying. The interior talks to staff, who are working. Same brand, different urgency.
  • Wireframed the staff screens first and the landing page last, so the promises on the site were ones the product could keep.
  • Prototyped the reservation-to-seating flow in Figma to test whether a host could seat a walk-in without leaving the floor plan.

Why it looks like this

Hospitality software usually borrows its palette from accounting software. I kept warmth and food photography on the owner-facing surface because the buyer is choosing a partner for their restaurant, not a database — and then dropped almost all of it inside the product, where the same warmth would slow a server down.

What I would change

The landing page carries a lot of decoration for a page whose real job is to make an owner trust it with their floor. I would test whether appetite or evidence converts better in the first screen.

Movie Reservation

Mobile app · UI and prototype

Booking a seat is a spatial problem on the smallest canvas you get. The seat map has to be readable at a glance, thumb-reachable, and honest about what is already gone.

Role
Complete UI and interactive prototype
Dates
05/2025 – 06/2025 · 2 months
Platform
Mobile · iOS frame
Screens
Browse, film detail and showtimes, seat selection, mobile ticket
Tools
Figma

Draft copy — edit freely

The hard screen

Everything in this app is easy except seat selection. It is a dense spatial map with three states that must be distinguishable instantly, inside a thumb's reach, on a surface where the poster art is also shouting. Get it wrong and people pick the wrong seat and blame you at the door.

Decisions

  • Dark surface throughout, so the poster carries all the colour and the seat map stays the only high-contrast information on screen.
  • Three seat states separated by hue and fill, not by shade alone, so the map still reads for someone who cannot rely on colour.
  • The curved screen line at the top orients the whole map instantly — you know which way you will be facing before you read a single label.
  • Summary bar pinned to the bottom with date, section, seat and total, so the commitment is always visible next to the Buy control.
  • The ticket screen is designed as the object you hold up at the door: barcode, seat, time, nothing competing.

What I would change

The showtime chips are staggered for visual rhythm, which costs scanability. A plain row grouped by time of day would be easier to read and less interesting to look at, and I now think easier wins here.

WishKart

Greeting-card platform · web

People do not arrive wanting a template. They arrive because someone they love has a birthday tomorrow. The navigation should start where they actually are.

Role
UI/UX Designer
Platform
Responsive web
Screens
Home and occasion picker, template browse, testimonials, contact
Tools
Figma, Illustrator, Canva
Note
Testimonials in the mockup are designed placeholder content, not real quotes

Draft copy — edit freely

The job

This is an emotional task with a hard time limit. Someone is making a card for a proposal or a graduation, they are in a particular mood, and they want to be finished in about two minutes. The interface has to match the mood without ever becoming the reason it took longer.

Decisions

  • Occasion-first navigation instead of template-first. People know the occasion before they know what they want it to look like, so the first choice on the page is the one they have already made.
  • Illustration rather than photography, because a photographed couple is somebody else's proposal and an illustrated one can be theirs.
  • The whole flow is three steps deep at most: occasion, template, personalise. No account required before the fun part.
  • Warm gradient held to the marketing surface. The editor itself needs to be quiet so the card being made is the loudest thing on screen.

What I would change

The occasion grid is six equal tiles, which quietly claims all six matter equally. Birthdays are almost certainly most of the traffic, and the layout should say so.

DualSense

Self-directed study · product card

An exercise in how far an object can push into a frame before the price and the primary action lose their place in the hierarchy.

Role
Concept and visual design — self-directed
Platform
Web · single product card composition
Focus
Visual hierarchy, depth without shadow, colour restraint
Tools
Photoshop, Figma
Note
Study piece. DualSense is a Sony product; this is not commissioned or affiliated work

Draft copy — edit freely

What I was testing

Product cards usually solve depth with a drop shadow and solve emphasis with a brighter button. I wanted to see whether the composition could carry both on its own.

Decisions

  • The ghosted word CONSOLE repeats behind the object. It builds three layers of depth — type, panel, product — without a single shadow.
  • The controller breaks the panel edge, so the object owns the frame instead of sitting inside a container.
  • Palette held to the controller's own two tones, which leaves the Add-to-cart button as the only saturated element competing with the product.
  • Price sits directly under the name at a size the eye reaches second, before the description and before the colour swatches.

What I would change

Add to cart and Add to wishlist are almost the same weight, so the card has two primary actions and therefore none. The wishlist control should drop to a quiet outline.

How I work

技

Six tools and one order of operations. The order matters more than the tools — I have redrawn a screen because the flow was wrong far more often than because the pixels were.

  1. 一

    Map the flow

    Before any screen exists, work out who touches the product, in what order, and where they are standing when they do it. Most bad screens are good screens in the wrong place.

    Service map
  2. 二

    Wireframe

    Grey boxes, real words. Deciding hierarchy while it is still cheap to be wrong, and writing the actual labels rather than lorem, because label length is a layout constraint.

    Low-fidelity screens
  3. 三

    Prototype

    Wire it up so it can be used rather than described. A flow that reads fine as a flat file often turns out to have one screen too many.

    Clickable file
  4. 四

    Interaction and states

    The states nobody asks for and everybody meets: loading, empty, error, disabled, too-long-name. Design them or inherit them.

    Motion and edge cases
  5. 五

    Responsive

    Take it down to a phone and back up again. Anything that only survives at one width was never finished.

About

人

I am a computer science undergraduate who designs. That combination is the useful part: I can sit in a conversation about what is actually buildable and not lose the argument about what it should feel like.

I came to interface design through drawing. I paint and sketch, and the habit that carried over was not style — it was patience with the boring middle of a piece, the part where you have committed to a composition and have to make it work. Screens have that same middle.

Most of my work so far has been on products for people doing a job under some kind of pressure: a restaurant during service, someone booking before the film starts. I would rather design the difficult screen than the beautiful one, though I would like the difficult screen to also be beautiful.

I am in my third year at St. Xavier's in Kathmandu, looking for an internship or junior role where I can work on real products with people who will tell me when I am wrong.
